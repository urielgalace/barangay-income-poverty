Methodology

How the estimates are made

The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) publishes income and poverty figures for regions and provinces, but not for individual barangays. This project uses machine learning to estimate how income and poverty differ among barangays, and then adjusts those estimates so that they agree with the official figures.

The approach in four steps

  1. Start with official figuresThe PSA’s published income and poverty figures set the level for each region and province.
  2. Train the modelsMachine learning models learn how satellite imagery and census data relate to income and poverty in places where both are known.
  3. Estimate every barangayThe trained models use each barangay’s own imagery and census data to estimate how it compares with other places.
  4. Match the official totalsThe estimates are adjusted so that barangays add up to the official figure for their area, and each receives a margin of error.

The idea rests on a simple observation. Two barangays in the same municipality can look very different from above: one may have dense housing and bright lights at night, and the other scattered homes and little light after dark. These visible differences are related to living standards. Where door-to-door surveys have measured poverty, a model can learn that relationship and then apply it to barangays that have not been surveyed.

The models do not decide how wealthy or how poor a province is. Official figures determine that. The models determine only how those totals are divided among barangays.

How the official figures are prepared

Household income. FIES publishes coefficients of variation in Table 3, with a median of 3.9% and a highest value of 14.7% in Samar. No figure is unusable, so income levels are kept as published.

Provincial poverty. The PSA reports four provinces at under 0.05% poverty for 2025: Ilocos Norte, Aklan, Nueva Ecija and Zamboanga Sibugay. The last two were 10.2% and 24.2% in 2023. A Fay-Herriot model on the log-odds scale combines each direct estimate with what the 2023 level and the regional change imply, trusting the direct estimate in proportion to its precision. Each region is then shifted so that its total matches the PSA’s. The eight municipalities of the Special Geographic Area have no published row, so their level is the official BARMM total minus its listed provinces. This choice is debatable, and an out-of-time test shows no clear gain from it.

City and municipal poverty. 1,597 cities and municipalities use their 2023 small-area estimate, shifted on the log-odds scale until their province reaches its 2025 figure. Highly urbanized cities use their own 2023 figures, smoothed against their earlier record. Manila uses its 14 sub-municipalities. Nine places with no official sub-area figure use a machine learning model trained on differences among municipalities.

The machine learning models

Two models produce the barangay estimates. They differ because the available data differ: income is published only for large areas, whereas poverty has been measured barangay by barangay in parts of the country.

How the algorithms work

Gradient boosting. A decision tree makes a prediction by asking a short series of yes-or-no questions, such as whether a barangay is brighter at night than its municipal average. Gradient boosting builds many small trees in sequence, with each new tree correcting the errors left by the trees before it. The final prediction adds together the small contributions of all the trees. This allows the model to capture thresholds and combinations of inputs that a simple formula would miss. The poverty model uses 300 trees, and the income model uses 200.

Ridge regression. Ridge regression multiplies each input by a weight and adds the results, with a penalty that keeps any single weight from growing too large. It is less flexible than gradient boosting, but it tends to behave more predictably for places unlike those in its training data.

Averaging. Gradient boosting involves random choices, so two runs on the same data give slightly different answers. Each model therefore averages ten runs with different random seeds, which reduced this variation about threefold without changing the held-out scores.

The household income model

FIES reports average income for provinces and some cities, so no income data exist for individual barangays. The model therefore learns across larger areas. For each province and city in 2021, 2023 and 2025, it learns how the area’s average income compares with the national average in the same year, which removes the effect of general income growth. Its inputs are the 99 measures above, averaged across the area’s households, plus the area’s estimated poverty rate.

The model is then applied to each barangay’s own measures and estimated poverty rate. This step assumes that the relationship seen across provinces and cities also holds across barangays. To limit the risk, each input is capped at the range seen in training, so that a barangay more extreme than any province or city cannot produce an extreme prediction.

The final model blends two algorithms equally. Gradient boosting explained more of the differences among held-out areas overall (R² of 0.70, against 0.64), while ridge regression ranked the held-out Metro Manila cities better (a rank correlation of 0.47, against 0.14). The blend scored higher than either, with an R² of 0.72.

The poverty model

The only published barangay-level poverty data found are reports from the Community-Based Monitoring System (CBMS), in which households were surveyed door to door. The 25 reports cover 6,618 barangays, but they date from 2005 to 2018 and use different local poverty lines, so their levels cannot be compared with today’s figures or with one another.

The model therefore learns only relative differences. For each surveyed barangay, it learns how far the barangay’s poverty rate sits from the average in its municipality, given how far its inputs sit from the municipal average. This approach assumes that the pattern of poverty within a municipality changes slowly, even when overall levels change. Training uses imagery from 2021, the earliest complete year, and the final estimates use imagery from 2025.

Matching the official totals

The models’ raw predictions are then adjusted so that barangays add up to the official figure for their area. The adjustment preserves the order of barangays within an area and changes only the overall level.

A simplified example. Consider a municipality with three barangays of equal population and a poverty rate of 20%. The model gives each barangay a relative score, and all three scores are shifted by the same amount (−1.48) until the average reaches 20%. The shift is applied to log-odds, a transformation of a rate that keeps every result between 0% and 100%.

Actual calculations weight each barangay by its population. Income is adjusted in the same way: every barangay in a province or city is multiplied by the same factor, so that the household-weighted average equals the FIES figure.

Margins of error

Each estimate carries a 90% interval. For household income, the interval combines the income model’s error on held-out areas with the effect of the error in the barangay’s poverty estimate. For poverty, it combines three sources of error: the uncertainty in the official area figure, the error in the municipal pattern, and the error in the barangay pattern on held-out surveys, which is larger in rural barangays than in urban ones.

These intervals are approximate, because the parts were tested separately and the whole was not. On held-out surveys, the barangay part of the poverty interval contained the surveyed value 92% of the time, close to its 90% target.

The comparison shows what the models add over simpler approaches. For income, the poverty rate alone reaches an R² of 0.60, and the final model raises this to 0.72. For poverty, nighttime lights alone reach a rank correlation of 0.42, close to the final model’s 0.46, so the gain is real but modest. The gain is larger in the five surveyed cities (0.42, against 0.28), although scores for individual cities vary. Training directly on barangays also outperforms applying a model trained on municipal figures (0.46, against 0.41).

Two further checks support the models. When a province and its cities were set aside, the income model recovered how much wealthier a city is than the rest of its province with a correlation of 0.53, and its typical error was about 34% of the error from assuming no difference. The final poverty model also matched the PSA’s small-area estimates in 59 provinces that contributed no surveys, with a rank correlation of 0.67. Because the small-area estimates share some inputs with the model, this is supporting evidence rather than an independent test.

What the tests cannot show

  • The surveys are old and partial. They cover 13% of the population in 11 of 18 regions, and 96% of the surveyed barangays come from reports begun by 2008. Accuracy for 2025 itself cannot be measured.
  • Barangay income has no direct test, because no barangay-level income data exist.
  • The models tend to underrate very wealthy, low-density neighborhoods. For example, Forbes Park ranks 20th of 23 barangays in Makati for income.

A separate script recomputes every published figure from the source files and labels what each check can show.

How the data were collected

The steps below retrace how the data were gathered. Most of the data were gathered on 30 October 2026, and some in June and July. The version history lists each date.

Official statistics from the PSA

psa.gov.ph sits behind a human check that blocks scripts, so each workbook was downloaded by hand in an ordinary browser. That includes the 2025 Full Year Official Poverty Statistics released on 24 August 2026, FIES 2025 preliminary tables 1, 3, 4 and 8, the 2023 poverty workbook, and the municipal small-area estimates for 2018, 2021 and 2023.

The 2025 workbook lists provinces, NCR districts and only two cities, so the 2023 workbook supplies city figures and the 2018 round.

The 2024 census from OpenSTAT

The PSA’s OpenSTAT database has a public PXWeb API that scripts can reach. One query per region returns population, household population and households for every barangay.

curl -X POST "https://openstat.psa.gov.ph/PXWeb/api/v1/en/DB/1A/PO_2024/<table>.px" \
  -d '{"query":[...],"response":{"format":"csv"}}'
Current geography from the PSGC

The PSGC 3Q 2026 datafile gives 42,010 barangays and 1,642 cities and municipalities. Its Summary of Changes is the PSA’s own record of merges, new barangays and renames. It gave 19 merges and 5 new barangays since the boundary layer was drawn.

Boundaries, and matching them to current codes

The newest barangay polygons in usable form are 2023 PSGC shapefiles: 42,048 polygons. Every polygon had to be matched to a current barangay, and codes alone are unsafe. Manila’s boundary code ...257 is Barangay 257, but the same PSGC correspondence code is Barangay 256. Maguindanao’s codes were reassigned. Name voting alone swaps Caloocan and Pasay, which both have Barangays 1 to 188.

Municipalities therefore match on code first, checked by overlap in barangay names, and barangays match on name within the municipality.

Households matched within each city

The census household tables use different names from the PSGC, and a barangay name with the same population can repeat in another city or municipality. Matching runs in order: city, name and population; then name and population where unique; then population alone where unique. Merged barangays add up their old parts. Households now equal the census in all 18 regions, 29,665,206 nationally.

Door-to-door poverty from CBMS reports

Reports for 20 provinces and 5 cities, dated 2005 to 2018, were read from PDF with pdftotext -layout, and then matched to the polygons by name within the municipality: 6,618 barangays in all. Each figure is the share of households below the poverty threshold.

Satellite and map data from Google Earth Engine

Each polygon’s average was taken for 2018, 2021, 2023 and 2025, simplified by at most 11 m. Open sea, which GHSL codes as -200, is masked out. Each year takes about 10 to 15 minutes with 10 workers, and runs resume where they stopped.
