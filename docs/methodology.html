Methodology

The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) publishes income and poverty figures for regions and provinces, but not for individual barangays. This project uses machine learning to estimate income and poverty for these barangays, then adjusts the estimates to align with official figures.

Overview of the methodology

  1. Start with official figuresThe PSA’s published income and poverty figures provide the basis for each region and province.
  2. Train the modelsMachine learning models identify how satellite imagery and census characteristics are associated with income and poverty in these areas.
  3. Estimate every barangayThe models apply these relationships to each barangay’s satellite imagery and census data to estimate how its income and poverty levels compare with those of other areas.
  4. Match the official figuresThe estimates are adjusted to align with official figures, and each estimate is assigned a margin of error.

How the data are processed

Household income. Table 3 of the FIES reports coefficients of variation with a median of 3.9% and a maximum of 14.7% in Samar. All income figures are retained as published.

Exclusive villages. Satellite imagery cannot capture the incomes of exclusive gated villages, so the models cannot estimate them reliably: Forbes Park, for example, ranked 20th of Makati’s 23 barangays. For six barangays that consist of such villages (Forbes Park, Dasmariñas, and Urdaneta in Makati; New Alabang Village, which contains Ayala Alabang, in Muntinlupa; Wack-Wack Greenhills in Mandaluyong; and White Plains in Quezon City), household income is set by hand to the average income of the richest tenth of households in their city, as reported by the FIES 2025. The remaining barangays in each city are scaled down so that the city average still matches the official figure. Poverty estimates are unchanged.

Provincial poverty. The PSA reports poverty rates below 0.05% for four provinces in 2025: Ilocos Norte, Aklan, Nueva Ecija, and Zamboanga Sibugay. However, the latter two provinces recorded substantially higher rates in 2023, at 10.2% and 24.2%, respectively.

To account for potential sampling variability, a Fay-Herriot model on the log-odds scale combines each province’s direct estimate with a prediction based on its 2023 poverty rate and the change observed in its region. The weight assigned to the direct estimate depends on its statistical precision. Provincial estimates are subsequently adjusted so that their population-weighted averages match official regional figures.

For the eight municipalities comprising the Special Geographic Area, which do not have separately published estimates, poverty levels are derived from the difference between BARMM’s official total and those of its listed provinces.

This approach involves methodological tradeoffs, and an out-of-time validation test does not establish a clear improvement from smoothing.

City and municipal poverty. For 1,597 cities and municipalities, the estimates start with the PSA’s 2023 small-area figures. These are adjusted on the log-odds scale to match provincial poverty rates in 2025. Highly urbanized cities use their own 2023 figures, smoothed using earlier observations. Manila is divided into its 14 sub-municipalities. For nine areas without official sub-area estimates, a machine learning model is used to estimate differences across municipalities.

Estimating income and poverty using machine learning

Two models are used to produce barangay-level estimates, one for household income and another for poverty. They are developed separately because the available training data differ. Income statistics are published primarily for larger geographic areas, while poverty has been measured at the barangay level in selected parts of the country.

Data used to train the models

Both models use 99 measures describing each geographic area. Most are derived from satellite imagery, while the remainder come from maps and the 2024 census.

Nighttime lights (4 measures). These capture nighttime brightness, including brightness relative to population and built-up area, which can indicate electrification and economic activity.

Buildings and settlements (15 measures). These include building counts and heights, built-up area and volume, residential density, roads, and green space. Together, they describe the density and physical characteristics of settlements.

Land cover and temperature (5 measures). These measure vegetation, built-up areas, water, greenery within developed areas, and daytime land surface temperature. They capture differences in land use and the built environment.

AlphaEarth embeddings (64 measures). These are numerical representations of land surface characteristics generated by Google’s AlphaEarth model. They capture geographic patterns that may not be represented by conventional satellite measures.

Terrain and accessibility (4 measures). These include elevation, slope, and travel times to the nearest city and health facility, which help capture geographic isolation.

Population and census characteristics (7 measures). These include population density, household size, urban or rural classification, the proportion of people living outside households, and crowding.

To assess the relative importance of these inputs, each group of variables is randomly rearranged in data excluded from training, and the resulting reduction in predictive performance is measured. Larger reductions indicate that the model relies more heavily on that group.

For the poverty model, building and settlement characteristics contribute most to predicting differences within municipalities. For the income model, which is trained on provinces and cities, the most important inputs are land cover and temperature, estimated poverty rates, and AlphaEarth embeddings.

How the machine learning algorithms work

Gradient boosting. A decision tree generates predictions by applying a sequence of yes-or-no rules, such as whether a barangay has brighter nighttime lighting than its municipal average. Gradient boosting combines many small decision trees, with each successive tree attempting to correct errors made by the preceding ones. The final prediction combines their contributions.

This approach allows the model to identify nonlinear relationships and interactions among variables that may not be captured by simpler methods. The poverty model uses 300 trees, while the income model uses 200.

Ridge regression. Ridge regression estimates an outcome by assigning weights to the input variables and combining their contributions. It also imposes a penalty on large coefficients to reduce overfitting. Although less flexible than gradient boosting, it can produce more stable predictions for areas whose characteristics differ from those represented in the training data.

Averaging. Because gradient boosting involves random elements, predictions can vary slightly across repeated runs. To reduce this variation, each model averages predictions from ten runs using different random seeds. This procedure reduced variation across runs by approximately two-thirds without affecting performance on held-out data.

Estimating household income

The FIES publishes average annual family income for provinces and selected cities, but not for individual barangays. Consequently, the income model is trained using data from larger geographic areas.

This site reports these figures as household income per month, dividing the annual figure by 12. A household can include more than one family, so the two measures differ slightly.

For each province and city in 2021, 2023, and 2025, the model estimates household income relative to the national average for that year. Expressing income in relative terms accounts for changes in national income over time.

The model uses the 99 geographic and census measures, averaged across households within each area, together with the area’s estimated poverty rate.

These relationships are subsequently applied to individual barangays using their own characteristics and estimated poverty rates. This requires assuming that relationships observed across provinces and cities also apply within them.

To limit extrapolation beyond the training data, each input is restricted to the range observed during training. This reduces the risk that barangays with characteristics more extreme than those of any training area will generate unusually extreme predictions.

The final model combines ridge regression and gradient boosting with equal weights. Gradient boosting explains more variation in income across held-out areas, achieving an R² of 0.70, compared with 0.64 for ridge regression.

However, ridge regression performs better at ranking held-out cities within Metro Manila, with a rank correlation of 0.47, compared with 0.14 for gradient boosting.

Combining the two models produces better overall performance than either model individually, with an R² of 0.72.

Estimating poverty

The available barangay-level poverty data come from published reports of the Community-Based Monitoring System (CBMS), which collected information through door-to-door household surveys.

The 25 reports cover 6,618 barangays. However, they were conducted between 2005 and 2018 and used different local poverty thresholds. Consequently, their reported poverty levels are not directly comparable across surveys or with current official figures.

The model therefore focuses on relative differences in poverty within municipalities rather than absolute poverty levels.

For each surveyed barangay, it measures how far the barangay’s poverty rate differs from its municipal average and estimates how that difference is associated with deviations in satellite and census characteristics.

This approach assumes that the relative distribution of poverty within municipalities changes more slowly than overall poverty levels.

The model is trained using satellite imagery from 2021, the earliest year with complete coverage, and subsequently applied to 2025 imagery to produce the final estimates.

Benchmarking the estimates against official figures

The models initially produce estimates based on the relationships identified during training. These estimates are then adjusted to ensure consistency with official figures for larger geographic areas.

The adjustment preserves the relative ordering of barangays within each area while changing their overall levels.

A simplified example. Consider a municipality consisting of three equally populated barangays, with an official poverty rate of 20%. The model assigns each barangay a relative poverty score. These scores are then shifted by the same amount, −1.48, until their average corresponds to the municipality’s official poverty rate.

The adjustment is performed on the log-odds scale, ensuring that all resulting poverty rates remain between 0% and 100%.

Household income estimates are adjusted similarly. Each barangay within a province or city receives the same multiplicative adjustment so that the household-weighted average matches the official FIES figure.

Margins of error

Each barangay estimate is accompanied by a 90% uncertainty interval.

For household income, the interval incorporates prediction errors observed in held-out areas, together with the uncertainty associated with estimated barangay poverty rates.

For poverty, the interval accounts for three sources of uncertainty: the official estimate for the larger geographic area, the estimated distribution of poverty across municipalities, and prediction errors within barangays observed in held-out surveys. The last source of uncertainty is greater for rural barangays than for urban barangays.

These intervals are approximate because the individual sources of error were evaluated separately rather than through a single test of the entire estimation procedure.

In held-out surveys, the barangay component of the poverty interval contained the observed poverty rate 92% of the time, close to its nominal coverage of 90%.

Model validation and performance

Models can appear accurate when evaluated using the same data on which they were trained. To assess their ability to predict outcomes in areas not used during training, all reported performance measures are calculated using held-out data.

For the household income model, each province and its constituent cities were excluded from training together. For the poverty model, each of the 25 survey reports was excluded in turn.

The results also indicate how much predictive performance improves relative to simpler approaches.

For household income, a model using only poverty rates achieves an R² of 0.60, compared with 0.72 for the final model.

For poverty, nighttime lighting alone produces a rank correlation of 0.42, compared with 0.46 for the final model. The improvement is therefore relatively modest overall.

However, the difference is more pronounced in the five surveyed cities, where the final model achieves a rank correlation of 0.42, compared with 0.28 using nighttime lighting alone. Performance nevertheless varies across individual cities.

Training directly on barangay-level observations also produces better results than applying a model trained on municipal-level data, with rank correlations of 0.46 and 0.41, respectively.

Two additional tests provide supporting evidence.

First, when a province and its cities are excluded from training, the income model is able to estimate income differences between cities and the rest of their province, achieving a correlation of 0.53. Its typical prediction error is approximately 34% of the error obtained by assuming that no income difference exists.

Second, the poverty model’s predictions are compared with the PSA’s small-area estimates in 59 provinces that did not contribute training surveys. The resulting rank correlation is 0.67.

Limitations of the tests

  • The survey data are dated and geographically incomplete. They cover 13% of the national population across 11 of 18 regions. Moreover, 96% of surveyed barangays come from reports initiated by 2008. Consequently, the model’s accuracy for 2025 cannot be evaluated directly.
  • Barangay income estimates cannot be validated directly. No observed barangay-level income data are available for comparison.
  • The models tend to underestimate income in wealthy, low-density neighborhoods. For example, Forbes Park is ranked 2th out of 23 barangays in Makati in estimated income.

Data collection

The following sections document the procedures used to obtain and prepare the underlying data. Most data were collected on October 30, 2026, with additional collection conducted in June and July. The version history documents the relevant dates.

Official statistics from the PSA

The official statistical workbooks were downloaded manually from the PSA website. These include the 2025 Full Year Official Poverty Statistics, released on August 24, 2026; preliminary FIES 2025 Tables 1, 3, 4, and 8; the 2023 poverty workbook; and the municipal small-area estimates for 2018, 2021, and 2023.

The 2025 poverty workbook reports figures for provinces, NCR districts, and only two cities. Consequently, the 2023 workbook is also used to obtain city-level estimates and figures from 2018.

2024 census from OpenSTAT

The PSA’s OpenSTAT database provides a publicly accessible PXWeb API, allowing census data to be retrieved programmatically.

One request per region was used to obtain barangay-level population, household population, and household counts.

Current geography from the PSGC

The third quarter 2026 Philippine Standard Geographic Code (PSGC) identifies 42,010 barangays and 1,642 cities and municipalities.

The PSA’s Summary of Changes documents geographic revisions, including mergers, newly established barangays, and name changes. Since the available boundary files were produced, 19 barangay mergers and five new barangays have been recorded.

Geographic boundaries and matching current codes

The most recent usable barangay boundary files are the 2023 PSGC shapefiles, containing 42,048 polygons.

These polygons were matched to the current barangay classifications. Matching required more than geographic codes alone because some codes have been reassigned or do not correspond directly to current barangay identifiers.

For example, Manila’s boundary code ending in 257 identifies Barangay 257, while the corresponding PSGC code identifies Barangay 256. Geographic codes were also reassigned in Maguindanao.

Matching based solely on names is similarly unreliable. Caloocan and Pasay, for example, both contain barangays numbered 1 through 188, creating the possibility of incorrect geographic assignments.

To address these problems, municipalities were first matched using geographic codes, with barangay name overlap used as a verification measure. Individual barangays were then matched primarily by name within their respective municipalities.

All 42,010 current barangays were successfully assigned to geographic boundaries, and the population represented by the polygons matches the official census total.

Matching households within each city and municipality

Barangay names in the census household tables do not always correspond directly to those in the PSGC. In addition, barangays with identical names and population counts may exist in different cities or municipalities.

The matching procedure therefore follows a sequence of increasingly flexible criteria: city or municipality, barangay name, and population; then name and population where the combination is unique; and finally population alone where the value is unique.

For merged barangays, household counts from the original geographic units are combined.

The resulting household counts match official census totals across all 18 regions, with 29,665,206 households nationally.

Door-to-door poverty data from CBMS reports

Published CBMS reports covering 20 provinces and five cities, dated between 2005 and 2018, were processed using pdftotext -layout.

Reported barangay figures were then matched to geographic boundaries using barangay names within each municipality, resulting in 6,618 matched barangays.

Each reported poverty rate represents the proportion of households falling below the applicable poverty threshold.

Satellite and geographic data from Google Earth Engine

Satellite and geographic measures were extracted for each polygon for 2018, 2021, 2023, and 2025.

Polygon boundaries were simplified by no more than 11 meters to reduce computational requirements. Open-sea cells, coded as −200 in the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL), were set to zero.

Meta’s Relative Wealth Index as an external comparison

Meta’s Relative Wealth Index, available at a spatial resolution of approximately 2.4 kilometers, was matched to barangay polygons to provide an additional comparison with the final estimates.

However, because the index is partly derived from satellite imagery, agreement between the two measures does not constitute fully independent validation.
